# Part 1: Strategy and background

## 1. The strategy in one minute

Unit 1 showed four things:
- every question comes from a slide,
- questions run in slide order,
- each question tests one fact, and
- the hard part is telling look-alike terms apart.

So:

1. **The PowerPoints are the list of what's tested.** Study every slide, including the "Text Alternative" slides at the back of each deck. Use the textbook only to look up specific things. SmartBook (extra credit) is your textbook reading.
2. **Bring the printed notes; add by hand only what's missing.** Printed notes are allowed now (her video call; no pictures, no computer). The printouts in `Printed Exam Notes/` have every slide in order, every figure in words, comparison tables and indexes (§6). Handwriting is your biggest time cost, so skip it unless something you'd need to look up isn't in them.
3. **Spend time where the points are.** Expect roughly 10 questions per chapter. The opening section of each chapter and the big comparison tables are the densest. Organism photo slides, uses and history are the thinnest.
4. **Keep notes in slide order** (the printed notes are, with the slide codes on each page in the page header), so during the exam you flip forward as the questions move through the deck.
5. **Test yourself instead of rereading,** and spread it out: answer each chapter's self-check questions (§13) when you finish the chapter, again the next day, and once more the day before the exam.

> **Why this works (research):** a major review of study methods rated self-testing and spacing study over several days as the two most effective techniques, and rereading and highlighting as among the least effective ([Dunlosky et al., 2013](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)). Another study found that students who expected an open-book test studied less and remembered less later ([Agarwal & Roediger, 2011](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/expectancy-of-an-open-book-test-decreases-performance-on-a-delaye/)). Your exams are open-note, but notes help most when you already know the material and only need to check a detail.

| **24 Flowering Plants: Structure and Organization** | The body of a flowering plant: its cell and tissue types, and its three organs (roots, stems, leaves). |
| **25 Flowering Plants: Nutrition and Transport** | How a plant gets minerals from soil, pulls water up from roots to leaves, and moves sugar around. |
| **26 Flowering Plants: Control of Growth Responses** | How plants respond to light, gravity, touch and day length without a nervous system, mainly using hormones. |
| **27 Flowering Plants: Reproduction** | How flowers make seeds and fruits, how seeds sprout, and how plants reproduce without seeds. |

### Six ideas that unlock the whole unit

**1. Plants have two kinds of "body" that take turns (alternation of generations).**

You know from Bio I that **haploid (n)** cells have one set of chromosomes and **diploid (2n)** cells have two. **Meiosis** turns a 2n cell into n cells, **mitosis** copies a cell without changing n or 2n, and **fertilization** joins two n cells into a 2n cell.

In animals, only eggs and sperm are haploid. **Plants have a whole multicellular haploid plant and a whole multicellular diploid plant, and they take turns:**

```
sporophyte (2n plant) --meiosis--> spores (n) --mitosis--> gametophyte (n plant)
        ^                                                        |
        |                                                  mitosis makes
     zygote (2n) <------- fertilization <------- sperm + egg (gametes, n)
```

- The **sporophyte** ("spore plant," 2n) makes **spores** by meiosis.
- A **spore** is not a gamete. It doesn't fuse with anything; it just grows into a new gametophyte.
- The **gametophyte** ("gamete plant," n) makes eggs and sperm by mitosis.
- The **dominant generation** is the bigger, longer-lived one, the plant you'd actually notice.

**2. Moving onto land is the story of Ch 23.** Each plant group added something that helped it live on dry land:

| Group (examples) | Vascular tissue (plumbing)? | Seeds? | Flowers & fruit? | Dominant generation | Needs outside water for fertilization? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Bryophytes** (mosses, liverworts, hornworts) | No, so they stay small and low | No | No | Gametophyte | Yes: sperm swim through a film of water |
| **Seedless vascular plants**: lycophytes (club mosses) and pteridophytes (ferns, horsetails) | Yes | No (spread by spores) | No | Sporophyte | Yes |
| **Gymnosperms** (pines and other conifers, cycads, ginkgo) | Yes | Yes, "naked" seeds, usually in cones | No | Sporophyte | No: pollen carries the sperm to the egg |
| **Angiosperms** (flowering plants) | Yes | Yes, inside a fruit | Yes | Sporophyte | No |

**3. A flowering plant's body is built from three tissue types,** all produced by **meristems**, zones of cells that keep dividing for the plant's whole life:
- **epidermal (dermal) tissue**, the outer "skin",
- **ground tissue**, the filler that does photosynthesis, storage and support, and
- **vascular tissue**, the plumbing.

These tissues make up the three **organs**: roots, stems and leaves.

**4. The plumbing has two parts:**
- **Xylem** carries water and minerals *up* from the roots. Its conducting cells are dead, hollow tubes.
- **Phloem** carries *sugar* from where it's made or stored (a **source**, such as a leaf) to where it's needed (a **sink**, such as roots or fruit), in any direction. Its conducting cells are alive.

**5. Plants coordinate with five hormones.** Each one is a chemical signal made in one part of the plant that changes what cells do elsewhere:

| Hormone | Its main job, in a phrase |
|---|---|
| **Auxin** | Growth direction; keeps side branches from growing |
| **Gibberellins** | Stem growth; waking seeds up |
| **Cytokinins** | Cell division; slowing aging |
| **Abscisic acid (ABA)** | The stress hormone: dormancy, closing leaf pores |
| **Ethylene** | A gas that ripens fruit and makes leaves drop |

**6. Flower → seed → fruit.** A flower's female part has an **ovary** containing **ovules**. After fertilization:
- each **ovule becomes a seed** (embryo + stored food + seed coat), and
- the **ovary becomes the fruit.**

Flowering plants use **double fertilization:** one sperm fertilizes the egg (making the zygote, which grows into the embryo), and the other combines with two extra nuclei to make the seed's food tissue (**endosperm**).

### Ch 23 Plant Evolution and Diversity

**What this chapter is about:** plants evolved from freshwater green algae. Life on land brought problems: drying out, getting water up a tall body, reproducing without water for sperm to swim in, and UV damage. Each major plant group solved more of them (see the table in §2, idea 2). The chapter walks through the groups in order and ends with flowers. **Understand**
- **Where plants came from:** freshwater green algae (**charophytes**). Shared with green algae: chlorophylls, starch storage, cellulose cell walls. The difference is that plants protect **and nourish** the embryo.
- **Problems of land and their solutions:**
  - drying out → **cuticle**,
  - gas exchange → **stomata**,
  - moving water upward → **tracheids/vascular tissue**,
  - UV damage → a **diploid** generation, whose second chromosome set can mask a single harmful allele.
- **The shift to a dominant sporophyte is a land adaptation.** Only sporophytes have vascular tissue, and the gametophyte shrinks and becomes dependent.
- **Bryophytes** have no vascular tissue, so they stay low, have no true roots, stems or leaves, and their **flagellated sperm swim in a film of water**.
- **Cooksonia** (an early vascular plant fossil) branched, which meant more sporangia and spores and indicates vascular tissue.
- **Megaphylls** capture more sunlight → more food → higher fitness.
- **Seeds** survive harsh conditions through dormancy, which is why seed plants dominate today. Seed plants keep their spores inside, and pollen carries the sperm, so **no outside water is needed** for fertilization.
- **Gymnosperm vs. angiosperm:** ovules not fully enclosed ("naked" seeds) vs. ovules enclosed in an ovary that becomes a fruit.
- **Flowers match their pollinators:**
  - wind-pollinated flowers aren't showy,
  - bird- and insect-pollinated flowers are colorful, and
  - night-blooming flowers are white or cream and fragrant.
- **Fungi and plants moving onto land** (learning objective M3-3): mycorrhizae show up in plant fossils, and fungi may have been instrumental in plants colonizing land (Ch 22 slide 7). Today most plants still depend on mycorrhizae (Ch 22 slide 50, Ch 24 slide 34).

**Understand**
- **Meristems let plants grow all their lives.** Apical meristems make the plant longer; lateral meristems (vascular cambium, cork cambium) make it thicker.
- **Girdling kills a tree** because the bark contains the phloem, so sugar can no longer reach the roots.
- **Annual vs. perennial:** annuals die after one season of making seeds; perennials invest in roots, stems and leaves to survive for years. The flower-inducing genes **LEAFY and Apetala 1** act as the switch between the two; blocking them in an annual makes it grow like a perennial.
- **Root hairs** add surface area for absorption. The **Casparian strip** forces water and minerals to pass through endodermal cells.

**Notes: look-up tables**
- **Primary meristems → tissues:** protoderm → epidermis; ground meristem → ground tissue (pith and cortex); procambium → vascular tissue (primary xylem and phloem).
- **Epidermis features:**
  - cuticle (prevents water loss)
  - root hairs (absorption)
  - trichomes (sun and moisture protection; discourage plant-eaters)
  - stomata (lower leaf surface)
  - periderm/cork from cork cambium in woody stems (cork contains suberin; gas exchange only at **lenticels**)
- **Ground tissue table:**
  - **Parenchyma:** least specialized, most abundant, photosynthesis, can divide.
  - **Collenchyma:** thicker primary walls, flexible support (the strings in celery).
  - **Sclerenchyma:** thick lignified secondary walls, mostly dead at maturity. **Fibers** are long (hemp, rope); **sclereids** are short (seed coats, nutshells).
- **Vascular table:**
  - **Xylem:** water and minerals up. **Tracheids** are tapered with pits; **vessel elements** are larger, with perforation plates, forming a continuous pipe.
  - **Phloem:** sucrose. **Sieve-tube members** have no nucleus and have sieve plates; **companion cells** have a nucleus and connect to them by plasmodesmata.
  - **Location:** vascular cylinder (root), vascular bundles (stem), veins (leaf).
- **Root zones, tip upward:** root cap (protects the root's apical meristem) → zone of cell division → zone of elongation → zone of maturation (root hairs, fully differentiated cells).
- **Root tissues, outside in:** epidermis → cortex (parenchyma; water and minerals move through it) → **endodermis** + Casparian strip → **pericycle** (makes lateral roots) → vascular cylinder.
- **Root types:** taproot (eudicots; stores food) vs. fibrous (monocots; anchors). Monocot roots have pith inside a ring of vascular tissue. Slide 30 says they don't undergo secondary growth, but the textbook (p. 437) says they undergo the same secondary growth as eudicot roots: follow the textbook (§11).

- **Stems:**
  - **Woody twig parts:** terminal bud with bud scales (each ring of scale scars = one year of growth), nodes, internodes, axillary buds, leaf scars, lenticels.

- **Leaf:**

### Ch 25 Flowering Plants: Nutrition and Transport

**What this chapter is about:** how a plant feeds itself.
- It builds itself mostly from CO₂ and water.
- It absorbs other elements from the soil as mineral ions.
- Water is pulled up through xylem by evaporation from the leaves.
- Sugar is pushed through phloem from where it's made to where it's needed.

**Understand**
- **Carbon, hydrogen and oxygen are about 95% of a plant's dry weight** (slide 3). CO₂ supplies the carbon and water supplies the hydrogen and oxygen. Everything else is absorbed as **mineral ions** through the roots.
- **An essential nutrient** has an identifiable role, can't be replaced by another nutrient, and without it the plant dies (slide 4). **Hydroponics** finds essential nutrients by leaving one out and watching growth suffer (slide 6).
- **Roots need oxygen** for cellular respiration (slide 5's figure shows oxygen entering roots). *(Textbook:)* that's why overwatering kills plants: water fills the soil's air spaces.
- **Water enters roots passively** (slide 19). The slide (and the textbook) word it as "when root cells' osmotic pressure is lower than the soil's," which means: when there's more water outside the root than inside, water moves in by osmosis, i.e., from higher to lower **water potential** (soil → root → stem → leaf → air, slides 31–33). **Minerals are taken up actively**, so the plant can concentrate them far above soil levels (slide 20).
- **Two pathways into the root** (slide 19; text alternative of slide 23): between cell walls until the **Casparian strip** forces everything through endodermal cells, or cell to cell through **plasmodesmata**. The Casparian strip also stops minerals leaking back out (slide 20).
- **Cohesion-tension (how xylem works, slides 37–39):** transpiration at the leaves creates tension that pulls a continuous column of water up from the roots. **Cohesion** keeps the column unbroken; **adhesion** holds it to the vessel walls. It's passive: the plant spends no energy. *(Textbook: at least 90% of the water roots absorb is lost by transpiration.)*
- **Root pressure** pushes xylem sap up from below and can cause **guttation**, water forced out at leaf edges (slides 35–36). *(Textbook: it isn't enough to lift water up tall trees.)*
- **Pressure-flow (how phloem works, slides 46–47):**
  - Sucrose is actively loaded into sieve tubes at the **source** (leaves). Slide 45: an ATP-driven pump pushes H⁺ out, and H⁺ flowing back in carries sucrose with it (co-transport).
  - Water follows by osmosis, building pressure.
  - The pressure pushes the sap to a **sink** (e.g., roots), where sugar is unloaded and used or stored.
  - Phloem sap can move up or down; xylem only moves up (slides 29, 43).
- **The cuticle and stomata work as a pair:** the waxy cuticle prevents water loss while stomata are closed (slide 39). *(Textbook: closing stomata saves water but stops CO₂ from entering, so photosynthesis slows.)*

**Notes: look-up tables**
- **Nutrients (slide 4):**
  - **Macronutrients:** C, H, O, **N**, P, K, S, Ca, Mg. **The slide leaves out N; the textbook's Table 25.1 includes it, so follow the textbook** (§11).
  - **Micronutrients:** Fe, B, Mn, Cu, Zn, Cl, Mo.
  - **NPK** = the fertilizer ratio (slide 6). The text alternative of slide 8 gives the roles: N → green leafy growth; P → root development; K → overall vigor. *(Textbook: N → nucleic acids and proteins; Mg → chlorophyll; Fe → cytochromes; beneficial nutrients such as silicon in horsetails.)*
- **Soil (slides 9–12, 14):**
  - **Contents:** mineral particles + decaying organic matter (humus) + living organisms + air + water.
  - **How it forms:** **mechanical** weathering (wind, rain, ice, rock grinding on rock) and **chemical** weathering (acid rain; lichens and mosses).
  - **Particles:** **sand** (largest, drains fast) > **silt** > **clay** (smallest, holds water). **Loam** combines sand's aeration with silt and clay's mineral retention.
  - **Humus:** decreases runoff, prevents leaching, makes soil more acidic, holds positive mineral ions; bacteria and fungi break it down and return nutrients.
  - **Organisms:** earthworms and ants mix and aerate soil; microorganisms decompose humus; soil bacteria make nitrogen available.
- **Cation exchange (slides 12–13):** clay and humus are negatively charged and hold Ca²⁺ and K⁺. The root releases **H⁺**, which swaps places with a mineral ion, and the root absorbs it. **CEC** is lower in sandy soils than in soils with clay and humus.
- **Soil profile (slides 15–16):** **A horizon** = topsoil (litter and most of the humus) → **B horizon** = subsoil (minerals leached down from above) → **C horizon** = weathered parent material (bedrock). The profile varies with parent rock and climate.
- **Erosion (slides 17–18):** water or wind carries soil away; plowing, overgrazing, land clearing and coastal storms make it worse. **Reduce it with** crop rotation, reduced tillage, companion crops, and trees (coastal erosion).
- **Order of mineral uptake across the membrane** (an M4 objective; slide 21 + text alternative of slide 23):
  1. An ATP-powered **proton pump pushes H⁺ out** of the cell.
  2. The resulting electrochemical gradient lets **K⁺ (and other positive ions) in through a channel protein**.
  3. **Negative ions (e.g., NO₃⁻) come in together with H⁺** through a carrier (co-transport).
  This use of an H⁺ gradient is **chemiosmosis**.
- **Nutrient helpers (slides 24–27):**
  - **Root nodules:** *Rhizobium* bacteria fix atmospheric nitrogen; the plant gives them carbohydrates.
  - **Mycorrhizae:** the fungus increases surface area for water and mineral uptake and breaks down organic matter; the root gives the fungus sugars and amino acids.
  - **Parasitic plants:** dodder taps its host *(textbook: through haustoria)*.
  - **Carnivorous plants:** sundew traps and digests insects (its sticky hairs release digestive enzymes).
- **Xylem vs. phloem (slides 28–30):** xylem = tracheids + vessel elements, water and minerals moving up; phloem = sieve-tube members (end walls = sieve plates, with plasmodesmata through them) + companion cells (supply proteins), sugar moving in any direction.
- **Water potential (slides 31–34):** higher pressure → higher water potential; more dissolved solute → lower water potential. **Turgor pressure** keeps cells firm; a wilted plant has lost it.
- **Stomata (slides 41–42):**
  - **How they work:** K⁺ is actively transported into the guard cells → water follows by osmosis → turgor rises → the stoma opens (H⁺ builds up outside as K⁺ moves in). K⁺ and water leave → it closes.
  - **Controlled by:** light (opens) and **ABA** (closes). *(Textbook adds: blue light acting through a flavin pigment, high CO₂ and water stress close them, plus temperature, humidity and a roughly 24-hour clock.)*
- **Evidence for phloem transport (slides 43, 48):** girdling makes the bark swell just above the cut because sugar collects there; radioactive tracers show phloem carries organic nutrients; **aphids'** mouthparts tap sieve tubes and are used to collect phloem sap.

### Ch 26 Flowering Plants: Control of Growth Responses

**What this chapter is about:** plants can't move away from danger or toward food, so they respond by changing how they grow.
- **26.1, hormones:** they detect a signal, relay it inside the cell, and respond, often using one of five hormones.
- **26.2, growth and movement:** growing toward or away from light, gravity or touch, and fast "turgor" movements like a Mimosa folding.

**Understand**
- **Signal transduction:** a **receptor** protein is activated by a signal → a **transduction pathway** of relay proteins amplifies and converts it → a **cellular response**, often a change in which genes are turned on.
- **Hormones** are made in one part of the plant and travel in the phloem or from cell to cell.
- **Short-term vs. long-term responses:** stomata opening and closing (short) vs. roots growing down and stems growing up (long).
- **How auxin bends a stem toward light ("acid growth"):**
  1. Light comes from one side, and auxin moves to the **shady side**.
  2. Proton pumps there push H⁺ into the cell wall.
  3. The wall loosens, water enters, and turgor rises.
  4. The shady-side cells stretch, and the stem bends toward the light.
- **Tropism vs. turgor movement:**
  - A tropism is **growth** toward or away from a **directional** stimulus.
  - A turgor movement is **not growth** and **doesn't depend on the stimulus's direction**: Mimosa folding when touched, the Venus flytrap closing, daily sleep movements.
- **Phytochrome:**
  - Pr absorbs red light in daylight and becomes Pfr (active); Pfr turns back into Pr in darkness or shade. This switching lets the plant detect day and night length.
  - Leaf shade from neighbors means more **far-red** light, so the plant spends energy growing taller to compete.
- **Photoperiodism:** flowering depends on the length of the **night**. Short-day plants are really long-night plants; long-day plants are short-night plants. A flash of light in the middle of the night stops a short-day plant from flowering and lets a long-day plant flower.
- **Circadian rhythm:** about 24 hours; it keeps going without outside cues (biological clock); **entrainment** resets it to daybreak.

**Notes: look-up tables**
- **Hormone table** (from slides 8–22 and text alternative slide 50). Where each is made: auxin in the shoot apical meristem (slide 8); cytokinins in dividing root tissue, seeds and fruits (slide 14); ABA in any green tissue (slide 17); ethylene is a gas made from the amino acid methionine (slide 20).

| Hormone | Main effects | Commercial use |
|---|---|---|
| Auxin (IAA) | Apical dominance (blocks side buds); root and fruit growth; prevents leaf and fruit drop; phototropism (shady-side cells stretch) | Weed killers (synthetic auxins like 2,4-D; Agent Orange); fruit without pollination |
| Gibberellins | Stem elongation; break seed and bud dormancy | Induce crop growth, larger plants |
| Cytokinins | Cell division and organ formation; slow aging (senescence); root nodules and galls; low levels in autumn → leaves change color | Keep flowers and vegetables fresh longer |
| Abscisic acid (ABA) | "Stress hormone"; starts and maintains seed and bud dormancy; **closes stomata**; ABA-insensitive corn shows vivipary | Thinning fruit |
| Ethylene (gas) | Abscission (via the enzyme cellulase); fruit ripening; inhibits side buds; suppresses stem and root elongation | Ripening fruit for market |

*Keep the commercial-use column: one short phrase per row (see §12 on uses).*

- **Auxin + cytokinin:**
  - Together they prevent senescence.
  - In tissue culture, their **ratio** decides whether you get a callus, roots, shoots or flowers (textbook Fig. 26.5).
- **How ABA closes stomata** (textbook Fig. 26.8): ABA binds a guard-cell receptor → Ca²⁺ enters → K⁺ channels open → K⁺, then water, leaves → the stoma closes.
- **Tropisms:**
  - **Phototropism:** stems positive; roots negative or insensitive; **phototropin** absorbs **blue** light.
  - **Thigmotropism:** tendrils of runner beans and morning glories coil around supports. **Thigmomorphogenesis** is a whole-shape change from touch or wind.
  - **Gravitropism:** stems negative, roots positive. **Statoliths** in **amyloplasts** of root-cap cells settle downward, and auxin redistributes.
- **Phytochrome:**
  - the Pr ⇄ Pfr cycle,
  - the short-day / long-day / day-neutral table, including what a night flash does, and
  - **etiolation** (textbook Fig. 26.17): with far-red light prevalent (shade) or in darkness, stems keep elongating and stay yellow.
- **Secondary metabolites:** defense chemicals against plant-eaters, parasites and competing plants, once thought to be waste.
- **Arabidopsis:** the model organism for plant genetics; small, short generation time (5–6 weeks), self-pollinates, small genome, no commercial value.

### Ch 27 Flowering Plants: Reproduction

**What this chapter is about:** the flowering plant's life cycle in detail:
- how a flower makes the male gametophyte (pollen) and the female gametophyte (embryo sac),
- pollination and double fertilization,
- how the embryo develops inside the seed,
- fruit types and how seeds are spread,
- how seeds sprout, and
- how plants reproduce without seeds (cuttings, runners, tissue culture).

Much of it builds on Ch 23's flower material, so use the §8 tables. Expect about 10 questions.

**Understand**
- **The sporophyte is dominant** and bears the flowers. It makes spores by meiosis, and the spores grow into tiny gametophytes by mitosis.
- **Pollination ≠ fertilization.** Pollination = pollen moving from anther to stigma. Fertilization happens later, after the pollen tube grows down the style to the ovule's **micropyle**.
- **Fruits** protect seeds and help spread them: by wind, by animals eating them, by hooks and spines catching on fur, or by forcibly ejecting them.
- **Germination needs:** **oxygen** (metabolism), **warmth** (enzyme activity) and **water** (to hydrate the cells).
- **Dormancy:** some seeds must go through a dormant period first. In temperate zones, cold weather breaks it. Fleshy fruits contain inhibitors so seeds don't sprout while still on the plant.
- **The sequence of germination** (learning objective M5-8; textbook 27.3 and slide 35's text alternative):
  - **All seeds:** the seed takes up water and the seed coat bursts.
  - **Bean (eudicot):** the root emerges first; the shoot is hook-shaped to protect the young leaves as it pushes through the soil; the two cotyledons supply energy until the leaves can photosynthesize, then shrivel.
  - **Corn (monocot):** the **coleoptile** sheath covers the first leaves and the **coleorhiza** covers the first root. These are discarded as the root grows down and the shoot grows up.
- **Asexual reproduction makes clones.** Tissue culture works because plant cells are **totipotent**.

**Notes: look-up tables**
- **Male gametophyte:** anther (4 pollen sacs) → microspore mother cell → **meiosis** → 4 microspores → **mitosis** → pollen grain (**tube cell + generative cell**) → the generative cell divides into **2 sperm** → mature male gametophyte.
- **Female gametophyte:** ovule → megaspore mother cell → **meiosis** → 4 megaspores, **3 die** → mitosis → **8 nuclei** = embryo sac: **egg + 2 synergids**, **central cell with 2 polar nuclei**, **3 antipodals**.
- **Double fertilization** (§8).
- **Flower terms:**
  - peduncle, receptacle
  - complete / incomplete
  - perfect (bisexual) / imperfect (unisexual)
  - **monoecious** (staminate and carpellate flowers on one plant) vs. **dioecious** (on separate plants; **holly**)
- **Embryo development (eudicot):** zygote → proembryo + suspensor → **globular** (outer cells become dermal tissue) → **heart** (cotyledons appear) → **torpedo** → mature embryo with **epicotyl**, **hypocotyl** and **radicle**.
- **Monocot vs. eudicot seed** (§8): the monocot cotyledon absorbs food from the endosperm; eudicot cotyledons store all the food.
- **Fruit types:**
  - **What a fruit is:** fruit = mature ovary; pericarp = exocarp, mesocarp, endocarp.
  - **By number of ovaries:** simple vs. aggregate (blackberry) vs. multiple (pineapple).
  - **Dry fruits:** dehiscent (legume) vs. indehiscent (grain).
  - **Named types** (defined in text alternative slide 53; examples from textbook Fig. 27.9): drupe (peach), berry (tomato), legume (pea), samara (maple).
- **Pollinators** (textbook Fig. 27A/27B; slides 18–19 are mostly photos):

| Pollinator | Typical flower |
|---|---|
| Bee | Yellow, blue or white (bees can't see red), with ultraviolet "nectar guide" markings |
| Butterfly | Often a composite flower with a broad landing area |
| Hummingbird | Red, slender tube, petals curved back, lots of nectar, little smell |
| Bat | Opens at night, white or pale, strong musky smell, large and sturdy |
| Wind | Not showy, sometimes no petals (Ch 23 slide 90) |

- **Asexual methods:**
  - stolons, rhizomes, tubers (potato "eyes" are buds)
  - **tissue culture:** meristem tissue on a medium with auxin, cytokinins and nutrients → **callus** → organogenesis → plantlet; the plants are clones
  - **cell suspension culture:** growing cells in liquid to extract chemicals, e.g., heart-disease drugs from **Digitalis**; avoids over-collecting wild plants
